Good to have you here.

I'm Kenji, and this is Counter.

I've been through what you've been saving, and one piece kept stopping me.
It's an argument about cities — about why some of them work and most of them don't.
The kind of argument that sounds like urban planning until you realize
it's actually about power.

You saved something from Works in Progress — a piece called The Death of the
Mixed-Use Street. It makes a case most urbanists would agree with.
But there's a version of the opposing argument that the piece never quite faces.

Let's get into it.

The claim at its strongest: when cities separate where people live from where
they work, shop, and gather, they produce places that require a car for every
errand, that empty out after 5pm, and that make it structurally impossible to
run into anyone you didn't already plan to see.

The author's evidence is specific. In cities that maintained mixed-use streets —
Tokyo, Amsterdam, pre-war New York — pedestrian activity stayed high,
small businesses survived, and the street itself became a form of infrastructure.
A place that does work.

In cities that zoned residences away from commerce — most of postwar America —
the street became a conduit. You pass through it. You don't use it.

The author's conclusion: mixed-use zoning isn't an aesthetic preference.
It's the mechanism that produces functional urban life.
The research behind this is substantial. Jane Jacobs made the case in 1961.
Sixty years of comparative urban data has largely held it up.

That's the claim. Take it seriously, because it earns it.

Now hold it. Because the strongest case against it doesn't come from the source.

The counterargument is this: mixed-use streets don't fail because planners
made bad aesthetic choices. They fail because land is an asset class.

When a neighborhood becomes desirable — walkable, activated, mixed — 
property values rise. Rising values attract institutional capital.
Institutional capital optimizes for yield, not use.
The small grocer gets replaced by a bank branch.
The bank branch gets replaced by a luxury residential lobby.
The thing that made the street valuable destroys the conditions
that made it valuable. Every time.

This isn't a planning failure. It's a market outcome.
And the mixed-use prescription, applied without addressing the capital
dynamics underneath it, produces gentrification as reliably as it produces
vitality. The two are the same process at different stages.

The author knows this. It surfaces briefly, then moves on.
That's where fact and opinion start to separate.

The fact: mixed-use environments produce measurable improvements in pedestrian
activity, small business survival, and street-level social density.
That's documented. It's not in dispute.

The opinion — stated with confidence but still an opinion — is that zoning
reform is the primary lever. That if you change the rules about what can be
built where, the streets follow.

What the evidence actually shows is more conditional.
Zoning reform produces mixed-use streets in places where land costs
allow small operators to survive. In markets where they can't,
you get the form without the function. Mixed-use buildings.
Not mixed-use streets.

The author's prescription is correct about the destination.
It's quieter about which cities can actually get there.

That's where the turn is.

The piece treats the problem as primarily regulatory.
Bad rules produced bad streets. Better rules will produce better streets.

But the cities that maintained functional mixed-use environments
didn't just have better zoning. They had land regimes that kept
speculative capital out of the street-level economy.
Community land trusts. Long-term commercial leases. Cooperative ownership structures.
The zoning was part of it. It wasn't the mechanism.

The author's framework optimizes for the right outcome.
It misidentifies what's actually blocking it.

So here's where it sits.

The disagreement isn't about whether mixed-use streets work.
They do. The disagreement is about what produces them and what destroys them.

Zoning can create the legal conditions for a mixed street.
It cannot create the economic conditions that keep it mixed
once it becomes desirable.

That gap — between the regulatory fix and the capital problem underneath it —
is where the tension actually lives. The piece is confident that the first
addresses the second. That's the opinion part. The evidence is more complicated.

That's Counter. Thanks for spending time with this one.

The detail that stayed with me: the bank branch replacing the grocer
replacing the lobby. That sequence happens so reliably it's almost
a law. Whether zoning can interrupt it is the open question —
and it's worth keeping.
